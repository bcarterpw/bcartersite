My Research


What is the proper role of government in a good society? I study how the American public wrestles with this ancient question in modern politics. Americans answer in strikingly different ways depending on their moral convictions, perceived self-interest, partisan loyalties, and their beliefs about the world. Government answers through policy, and those policies determine how society's benefits and burdens are distributed. Using survey experiments, field experiments, behavioral economics, and policy analysis, my work investigates how citizens think and vote, which policies pass, and ultimately how those policies affect ordinary people's lives.

— Current Projects —

  • The Burden of Taxes. Using methods from experimental economics, we test the burden hypothesis of redistribution preferences from earlier work (Carter et al, 2026). We find that people with low income vote for less redistribution when they must pay taxes to receive benefits. Removing the tax burden while keeping net benefits the same leads to robust support for redistribution among the poor, as rationality predicts.
  • Work Requirements. Safety-net programs like SNAP and Medicaid aim to promote self-reliance by requiring that beneficiaries work each month. As an ARTEMIS Fellow, I am working with J-PAL North America to craft a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) to identify why some citizens who meet the work requirements still lose their benefits.
  • Moral Conviction. People become more politically active and resistant to compromise when they feel morally convicted about an issue. For example, people often view harm done to others as a matter of "right" and "wrong". Using a series of survey experiments, I investigate how people's moral conviction and political behavior depends on who has been harmed (e.g., an ally, enemy, criminal, or victim), and who has been blamed.
