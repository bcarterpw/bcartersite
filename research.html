My Research


My research has two goals that reinforce each other: measuring how public policies affect people's lives, and understanding how citizens judge those policies. A policy can fail when eligible people misunderstand its rules, and a policy that works can lose support when voters misperceive its costs. Using field experiments, survey experiments, and policy analysis, I produce evidence that policymakers and practitioners can use.


— Current Projects —

  • Medicaid Work Requirements. Why do people who meet Medicaid work requirements still dropped from the program?

    As an ARTEMIS Fellow with J-PAL North America, I am designing an assessment study and randomized controlled trial that separates Medicaid enrollees who are not working from those who lose coverage to paperwork or confusion about the rules. The study can guide agencies on how to maintain patient coverage and avoid costly enrollee churn.

  • The Burden of Taxes. Does paying taxes turn the poor against policies that help them?

    Using economic experiments, we find that people with low income vote for less redistribution when they must pay taxes to receive benefits. Removing that burden while holding net benefits constant produces robust support, as rationality predicts. A policy's design shapes whether it keeps the support of the people it helps.

  • Rising Premiums. Whom do ACA Marketplace enrollees blame when their premiums go up?

    Using KFF panel data, I find that enrollees often assign blame by partisanship rather than by which officials made the decision. A follow-up study links survey responses to validated voter turnout in the 2026 midterms to test whether harm and blame change who votes.

  • Moral Conviction. When does harm make compromise harder?

    People become more politically active and less willing to compromise when they see an issue as a matter of right and wrong. In survey experiments, I test how conviction depends on who was harmed (an ally, an opponent, a criminal, or a victim) and who is blamed.
